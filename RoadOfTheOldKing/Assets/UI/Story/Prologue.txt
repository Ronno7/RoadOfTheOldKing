When I was young, my father would tell us tales of an old kingdom, one that ruled these lands long before our people.

He said they were a vast and powerful people whose roads, forts, and temples stretched far across the Broad Lands, farther than any kingdom that exists today.

They were ruled by an Old King, cunning from his many years upon the throne, yet his thirst for power was endless.

Wealth, land, and victory were no longer enough for him; he began to seek the power of the heavens themselves, believing that no man should kneel beneath the sun, the storm, or the gods who commanded them.

His priests raised great stones upon the hills, his smiths forged strange works of bronze, and his armies carried the sun-wheel upon their shields as they conquered one people after another.

At last the King ordered the construction of a great road leading into the sacred highlands, where the oldest stories claimed the sky once touched the earth.

What happened there has been lost to time. Some say he stole fire from the sun, others that he called down a storm no mortal could command, and still others say the gods themselves descended to punish him.

Whatever the truth, the kingdom fell in a single generation. Its cities burned, its warriors vanished into their barrows, and the King was never seen again.

The survivors scattered, and over the centuries new peoples settled among the ruins and eventually called the land Ardan.

Most now treat the old roads, standing stones, and burial mounds as little more than remnants of a forgotten age. To us, they were only stories; the old stones were places to sit, and the broken roads were simply the roads we used.

But my father always ended the story the same way: the Old King was never killed.

He was only buried, somewhere at the end of the road that once bore his name, waiting beneath the stones for the day the Broad Lands would remember him again.
